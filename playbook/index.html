Playbook

What happens in the ninety days after we close.

The same four phases every time, in the same order. The first one is the one most buyers skip, and it's the reason the other three work.

  1. Watch the business run

    Weeks 1 to 3 · On site

    We don't change anything. Max sits in the office and rides along with the crew. Every recurring task gets logged: what it is, who does it, how long it takes, what software or paper it touches, and what goes wrong when it's late. By the end of week three we have a list of a hundred or so tasks with hours attached.

    The staff know by then that we're not there to replace them. We're there to find out what they're sick of doing.

  2. Instrument it

    Weeks 3 to 5 · Mostly remote

    Bank and card feeds, the job calendar, the phone system, the inbox, and whatever the business uses for invoicing get connected to one dashboard. No new software for the staff to learn yet. The point is that the owner and the manager see, for the first time, daily cash, jobs booked, invoices outstanding, and response times on one screen.

    This is also when we set the baseline. Every automation we build next gets measured against the hours we logged in phase one.

  3. Automate the top five

    Weeks 5 to 11 · Build and deploy

    We rank the task list by hours and pick the top five. In a services business they're almost always some mix of quoting, invoicing and collections, scheduling and customer reminders, intake from the phone and inbox, and end-of-week reporting. Each one gets an agent that does the work and leaves a record a human can check.

    Every agent starts with a person approving its output. As the error rate drops, the approval step goes away for the routine cases and stays for the unusual ones.

  4. Hand it to a manager

    Weeks 11 to 13 · Transition

    A general manager runs the business from here. Often it's someone who already works there and has wanted the job. They get the dashboard, the agents, a weekly call with us, and a bonus tied to the numbers on the screen. We stay on as owners, keep the tooling current, and move on to the next company.

02Rules

What we don't do.

No rebrand

The name on the trucks stays. Customers bought from that name for twenty years.

No layoffs on day one

The office staff are the people who know how the business actually works. Automation takes the worst parts of their job. Headcount changes, when they happen, come through attrition.

No rip-and-replace

If the business runs on an old scheduling tool that works, we connect to it. We only replace software when it's the bottleneck.

No unattended money

Anything that sends an invoice, issues a refund, or moves cash gets a human approval until we have months of clean records.

03The kit

Tools we bring with us.

Everything below runs on Little Hammer Labs today or has shipped to a paying client. None of it is a demo.

Intake agent
Reads the inbox and voicemail transcripts, pulls out the request, the customer, and the urgency, and creates the job or the quote request in the system the business already uses.
Quoting and invoicing
Drafts quotes from a price book and past jobs, sends invoices when work is marked done, and chases unpaid ones on a schedule a human set.
Scheduling and reminders
Fills the calendar from confirmed quotes, sends confirmations and day-before reminders, and reschedules when a customer replies.
Transcription
Turns recorded calls, site visits, and meetings into searchable notes. Built for an architecture firm, runs on their own hardware, and processed 1,800 recordings in under an hour on its first real project.
Operations dashboard
Daily cash, bookings, receivables, and response times in one place, with a weekly summary written by an agent and read by a person.
Nightly operations agents
Eleven scheduled agents run our own company overnight: research, bookkeeping prep, outreach drafts, and reporting. This is the pattern we install in every acquisition.

Built with Anthropic Claude, open-weight models where privacy requires it, Python, and Node.js. Where it matters, the models run on the company's own hardware and customer data never leaves the building.

04Proof

Diehl Group Architects

A forensic architecture firm in Southern California. Their staff were billing professional rates to type out transcriptions of recorded site inspections, sometimes for days at a stretch.

We built them a transcription tool that runs on their own machines and doesn't send recordings to anyone's cloud. Six weeks from the first conversation to production. On its first real project it processed 1,800 recordings in under an hour. The firm estimates it saves more than $20,000 a year in labor across roughly a dozen projects, and it paid for itself on the first one.

"Sometimes we had guys billing one-fifty an hour sitting there typing out transcriptions all day. Little Hammer Labs built us a tool that handles the whole batch before lunch." Principal, Diehl Group Architects

That's one task in one firm. A company we own gets the top five.
