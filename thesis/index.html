Thesis

Small, boring, profitable, and run out of an inbox.

That's the business we want to buy. It has customers who come back, an owner who has done the same job for twenty years, and an office that runs on paper, phone calls, and one person's memory. The product is fine. The operation is where the money is left on the table.

01What we buy

Acquisition criteria

We'd rather pass on a hundred deals than buy one we can't run. These are the lines.

Revenue

$500K to $3M a year, with at least three years of history. Big enough to support a manager, small enough that institutional buyers aren't looking.
Earnings

$150K to $750K in seller's discretionary earnings. Consistent, not spiky. We'll look at a down year if the reason is obvious.
Business type

Services with repeat customers and a heavy office load. Home and property services, field services, small B2B services, and light professional services. Anything where quoting, scheduling, invoicing, and follow-up eat a few people's days.
Geography

Southern California first, because we show up in person for the first ninety days. Further afield once we have a manager we trust and a playbook that has survived contact with a real company.
Seller

An owner who wants a clean exit or a slow one. We're happy with a transition period and a seller note. We're not the right buyer for someone who wants to stay in charge.
Deal size

Roughly $400K to $2.5M enterprise value. Seller financing, SBA 7(a) where it fits, and investor equity through the holding company.
02What we avoid

Where we walk away

Key-person businesses

If the customers are really buying the owner, nothing we do with the back office matters. We look hard at whether the business survives the handshake.

Thin or lumpy margins

Restaurants, retail, and anything where one bad quarter is fatal. We want earnings that can carry debt service through a slow year.

Businesses that are already efficient

If the office is two people and good software, our edge is gone. We're paying for the gap between how it runs and how it could.

Turnarounds

We fix operations, not broken businesses. Declining revenue, legal trouble, or a lost major customer are someone else's project.

03Why now

Two things changed at once.

The office work got cheap to automate

Three years ago, automating a small company's quoting and invoicing meant a six-figure software project that nobody in the business could maintain. Today the same work is a few weeks for a competent builder and runs on a machine in the back room. The tools got good. Almost nobody in the small-business world has noticed yet, because the people who can build them don't want to run a plumbing company.

We do. Not the plumbing. The company.

The sellers showed up

A large share of American small businesses are owned by people near retirement age, and most of them have no succession plan. Brokers' listings are full of healthy companies at modest multiples because the buyer pool for a $1.5M services business is thin. Private equity can't make the math work at that size. Most individual buyers can't run the operation. We can, and we can make it cheaper to run on day ninety than it was on day one.

04Structure

How the company is put together.

Little Hammer Labs, LLC
The holding company, formed in California. It owns the operating entities, the automation tooling, and the playbook. Investors hold interests here or in a deal-specific vehicle, depending on the raise.
Operating companies
Each acquired business stays its own legal entity with its own books, bank accounts, insurance, and manager. A problem in one company stays in that company.
Shared services
The holding company provides the automation, bookkeeping systems, and reporting to every operating company. That's the part that gets cheaper with each acquisition.
Hold period
Indefinite. There is no fund with a term. We'll sell a company if someone offers a price that makes more sense than owning it, and not otherwise.
